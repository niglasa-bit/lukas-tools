6 Historic Stock Market Crashes & Recoveries: What 100 Years of Data Says Comes Next

The Crash Tracker: 6 / 6 recovered

Every major U.S. stock market crash of the last century — from 1929 to 2008 to 2020 — felt permanent on the day it happened, and every single one eventually recovered to new highs. The biggest threat to long-term investors is rarely the drop itself; it is reacting to the drop at the worst moment.

When the stock market falls hard and your portfolio turns red, every headline sounds like the beginning of the end. On 19 November 2026, the Dow dropped more than 1,100 points back below 50,000, the S&P 500 posted its sixth straight red session with over 90% of stocks down, and investors everywhere started asking the same question: what happens next?1

On this site, every money rule has to show its receipts. Instead of predictions, we put six of the most famous stock market crashes of the last hundred years on the board — how far they fell, how long recovery actually took, and what research says costs ordinary investors the most during red markets. Base rates since 1928: how normal are drops?

When your screen is red, your brain tells you something broke. Historical S&P 500 base rates going back to 1928 tell a calmer story:2

Decline sizeHow often it happensWhat history shows
5% pullback>90% of all calendar yearsNearly every year you invest has at least one 5% dip
10% correctionRoughly every 1.5–2 yearsMore than half stop here without reaching a 20% bear market
20% bear marketRoughly every 5 yearsAbout 1 in 4 years — painful, recurring, historically temporary

Volatility is not a malfunction in the stock market. It is the admission ticket for long-term equity returns. Roughly two-thirds of 5% pullbacks bounce back without ever reaching 10%, and more than half of 10% corrections recover without ever becoming a 20% bear market.2

Crash #1: 1929–1932 Great Depression

From September 1929 to mid-1932, the U.S. stock market fell roughly 86% over 33 brutal months.3 You have probably heard that it took 25 years, until 1954, to get back to the 1929 peak. On a price-only chart, that is true — and incomplete.

First, 1929 involved extreme leverage (investors routinely bought stocks with 10% down and 90% borrowed margin), zero federal deposit insurance, thousands of bank failures and a monetary contraction. Second, the 25-year figure ignores reinvested dividends and sharp deflation: adjusting for both puts the real breakeven closer to four and a half to five and a half years, and someone buying small amounts every month broke even even sooner.3

Verdict: Extreme Outlier. The harshest stress test in modern market history — and even it eventually recovered.

Crash #2: 1973–1974 Stagflation & Oil Shock

If 1929 was a cliff, the early 1970s were quicksand. An oil embargo, surging inflation, climbing interest rates and Watergate dragged the S&P 500 down roughly 48% over 21 grinding months.3 Price-only recovery took until 1980 (over seven years peak to peak); with dividends reinvested, the wait was roughly three and a half years.

Verdict: Slow Grind. Sudden crashes test your heart rate; slow, exhausting bear markets test your patience. Most people do not quit on day one — they quit in month eighteen, right before the turn.

Crash #3: 1987 Black Monday

On 19 October 1987, the S&P 500 dropped 20.5% in a single six-and-a-half-hour trading session — still the worst single day in U.S. market history. Peak to trough, the decline reached roughly 33.5% in three months, and front pages immediately revived 1929 comparisons.3

Yet the underlying economy did not collapse into a depression. The S&P 500 finished 1987 slightly positive for the calendar year and reached a new all-time high by July 1989 — roughly 20 months later. Anyone who sold in terror on Tuesday morning locked in the worst exit of the decade.

Verdict: Shock & Rebound.

Crash #4: 2000–2002 Dot-Com Bubble

Whenever a single booming technology theme dominates the news, 2000–2002 is the essential receipt. Over 31 months, the broad S&P 500 fell roughly 49%, while the tech-heavy Nasdaq collapsed 78%.3

Look at the recovery gap: the broad S&P 500 reclaimed its 2000 high by 2007 (roughly four and a half years after the bottom), whereas the Nasdaq did not reclaim its March 2000 peak until 2015 — fifteen years later. The internet really did transform the economy, yet concentrating in hype without diversification trapped investors for over a decade.

Verdict: Concentration Trap.

Crash #5: 2007–2009 Global Financial Crisis

Housing collapsed, Lehman Brothers failed and global credit froze. Over 17 months, the S&P 500 fell 57%, cutting portfolios more than in half.3 By March 2009, the consensus felt unmistakable: the financial system was broken for good.

Four years after that March bottom — by early 2013 — the S&P 500 was back at a record high, followed by one of history's strongest bull runs (multiplying more than fourfold from the 2009 trough over the following decade). Stopping automatic workplace retirement contributions in 2009 meant skipping buying the entire market at half price.

Verdict: Systemic Fear.

Crash #6: 2020 COVID-19 Crash

In early 2020, global lockdowns triggered a 34% S&P 500 plunge in just 33 calendar days — the fastest bear market on record.4 Millions of investors told themselves a reasonable-sounding sentence: "I'll sit in cash until things feel safe again."

Five months later, in August 2020, while pandemic uncertainty was still on every front page, the S&P 500 closed at a new record high.4 The market never waited for the headlines to feel safe.

Verdict: Fastest V-Shape.

The $3.8 trillion mistake: Morningstar & J.P. Morgan

If the broad market recovered from all six crashes, why do ordinary investors still lose money during downturns? Two primary datasets show the exact math. Evidence strength: strong.

First, Morningstar's Mind the Gap 2026 study measured real fund returns versus actual dollar-weighted investor returns over the ten years ended 31 December 2025. The funds earned 9.9% per year; the human investors inside those exact same funds earned 8.7% per year.5 That 1.2 percentage point annual behavior gap — buying after rallies and selling during drops — cost investors an estimated $3.8 trillion in foregone wealth.5

Second, J.P. Morgan Asset Management's Guide to Retirement (data through 31 December 2025) tracked a hypothetical $10,000 investment in the S&P 500 over 20 years (~5,000 trading days, Jan 2006 – Dec 2025):6

Strategy (2006–2025, $10,000 start)Ending balanceAnnualized return
Stayed fully invested$80,61911.0% / yr
Missed the 10 best days$35,8666.6% / yr
Missed the 20 best days—3.8% / yr
Missed the 30 best days—1.6% / yr

Why is dodging the worst days almost impossible? J.P. Morgan's data shows that 6 of the 10 best trading days occurred within two weeks of the 10 worst days — and 5 of those 6 came immediately after the worst days.6 As Peter Lynch famously noted: "Far more money has been lost by investors preparing for corrections, or trying to anticipate corrections, than has been lost in corrections themselves."7

What to actually do when everything is red (4-step system)

You do not need a market forecast to handle a red week. You need a calm system built before emotion takes over:

  1. Check your cash buffer before touching a single share. Money needed in the next three to five years does not belong in volatile stocks. If your emergency fund covers your bills, a red screen is uncomfortable — not an emergency.
  2. Keep your automatic contribution running. Fixed monthly investing acquires more shares automatically during red weeks without asking your nervous system for permission.
  3. Put a 48-hour rule on every portfolio change. If you feel the urge to sell everything, write your reason down and wait two full days. Panic peaks in minutes; clear decisions survive two nights of sleep.
  4. Zoom out your check-in schedule. Brad Barber and Terrance Odean's landmark study of 66,465 brokerage households found that the most active traders earned the lowest returns.8 Checking once a month instead of fourteen times a day protects your future self from your stressed-out present self.

If you are still following a written plan during a red week, you are already ahead of most of the crowd.
