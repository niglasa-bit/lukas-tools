The Richest Man in Babylon, Fact-Checked: 7 Rules vs Modern Evidence

Final score: 6½ / 7

A 1926 book of parables beats most money advice online. Six of its seven rules hold up against modern research and real history. One, "your home is a profitable investment", only half survives. Below: every rule, the evidence, and the one-line fix.

In the 1920s a Denver map maker, George S. Clason, wrote short money stories set in ancient Babylon. Banks and insurance companies handed them out as pamphlets, and in 1926 they became The Richest Man in Babylon.1 A hundred years later it still sells every year.

So we did what the book never had to survive. We put each of its seven "cures for a lean purse" against modern research, real history and one very famous bet. Each rule gets a verdict: true, false, or half true. This is the written version of our most-watched video, with every source listed at the end.

The real Babylon

Arkad, the book's richest man, never existed. But the real Babylon did think hard about money. Around 1750 BC the Code of Hammurabi capped loan interest at about 20% a year on silver and about 33⅓% on grain.2 Four thousand years ago someone looked at lending and decided there had to be a limit. Borrowed money has a price, and someone will always try to raise it.

Rule 1: "Start thy purse to fattening"

The most quoted line in the book: "A part of all you earn is yours to keep." Not a part of what's left. A part of all of it. One coin in ten, before anything else is paid.

Almost everyone agrees with this rule, and few follow it, because the brain wants the coin now. Homer had the fix three thousand years earlier: Odysseus wanted to hear the Sirens, so he had himself tied to the mast and told his crew to ignore whatever he said later.3 Don't rely on willpower. Decide once, in advance, and make saving automatic on payday.

Verdict: TRUE, with an upgrade. Automate it.

Rule 2: "Control thy expenditures"

The book says "necessary expenses" grow to match your income unless you stop them. In 1960 the historian C. Northcote Parkinson wrote the same thing down as a law: expenditure rises to meet income.4 Today we call it lifestyle creep. Each upgrade is small, none feels like a decision, and the raise disappears.

The fix is to change the unit. Thoreau wrote in 1854 that "the cost of a thing is the amount of what I will call life which is required to be exchanged for it."5 If you keep 20 an hour after tax, a 120 jacket costs six hours (Example). Some things are worth six hours. Many aren't.

Verdict: TRUE. Parkinson named the problem, and Thoreau gave the fix. Our free Future Cost Calculator does the hours math for you.

Rule 3: "Make thy gold multiply"

Every coin you keep should go to work, and its "children" should work too. We call it compound interest. The best real test was run by Benjamin Franklin. When he died in 1790 he left £1,000 each to Boston and Philadelphia, to be lent out and mostly left alone for two hundred years. By 1990 Boston's fund was worth about $4.5 million and Philadelphia's about $2 million.6

Franklin didn't pick a genius investment. He picked time. The rule of 72 shows why: divide 72 by your yearly return to get the years it takes to double. At 7%, money doubles roughly every ten years, and most of the growth arrives at the end.

Verdict: TRUE. If anything, the book undersells it.

Rule 4: "Guard thy treasures from loss"

Young Arkad gives a year's savings to a brickmaker buying jewels abroad. He comes back with glass. The lesson: don't invest in what you don't understand, on advice from people who don't understand it either.

Today the biggest thief is quieter: fees and overconfidence. In 2008 Warren Buffett bet a million dollars that a plain S&P 500 index fund would beat five funds of hedge funds over ten years. The index fund returned about 7.1% a year, the professionals about 2.2%.7 Buffett's own conclusion was that fees and clever activity ate the professionals' returns. We worked through what a "small" fee does over a lifetime in What a "Tiny" 1% Fee Really Costs You.

Verdict: TRUE. The danger just changed clothes.

Rule 5: "Make of thy dwelling a profitable investment"

Here Babylon meets a Nobel laureate, and it doesn't go well. Robert Shiller's long-run index of US home prices shows that over the hundred years to 1990, real home prices rose only about 0.2% a year.8 A house mostly kept its value. It did not multiply like Franklin's money.

In fairness to Clason, his real argument was about rent: an owner stops paying a landlord and can put that money to work. That part still holds, and a home gives you stability no fund can. But "profitable investment" is a stretch. A home is a place to live first and an investment second.

Verdict: HALF TRUE.

Rule 6: "Ensure a future income"

For most of history, the retirement plan was your children, or nothing. In 1889 Germany under Otto von Bismarck created the first state old-age insurance. The pension age was 70, not 65.9 Today most countries have a public pension, and it's a gift. But it's a floor, not a plan. Building your own income on top of it is exactly what the book asks for.

Verdict: TRUE.

Rule 7: "Increase thy ability to earn"

The rule people skip. If you're starting near zero, saving ten percent of very little is still very little. Double your income and the same ten percent doubles, with no extra willpower. At zero, the best investment is you.

Does more income actually make life better? In 2023 Daniel Kahneman and Matthew Killingsworth, who had published opposite answers, ran a joint study. For most people well-being keeps rising with income. The plateau appeared only in the unhappiest group, where money stops fixing what's wrong.10

Verdict: TRUE.

The final score

Babylon 6½ / 7
  1. Keep one coin in ten, automatically.
  2. Read prices in hours of your life.
  3. Let time do the heavy lifting.
  4. Guard against fees and cleverness, not just crooks.
  5. A home is for living first.
  6. The state pension is a floor, not a plan.
  7. At zero, invest in yourself.

Notice what the book doesn't say. No secret stock, no hack, no "rich in 30 days". Every rule is boring, which is exactly why it works, and exactly why most people skip it. Modern science didn't overturn Babylon. It mostly explained why Babylon was right: we're not bad with money for lack of information, but because the brain wants things now, prices hide their real cost, and clever-sounding people are convincing.

  2. Look at the last three things you bought, in hours.
  3. Set one automatic transfer on payday, even if it's small. That's rule one done for good.
