201 Studies, 0.1 %: Why Money Tips Don't Stick (and What Does)

Tips: 0.1 % · Systems: 37 % → 86 %

Researchers pooled 201 studies of financial education and found it explained about 0.1 % of the difference in what people actually do with money. Changing one default on a form did more. Knowledge isn't the missing piece. A system you set up once is.

You're surrounded by money tips. They're free, they're everywhere, and most of them are even true. So why do so many people follow the tips and still go nowhere? This is the written version of our video on tips versus plans, with every source at the end.

The number: 0.1 %

In 2014, three researchers did something slightly rude. Daniel Fernandes, John Lynch and Richard Netemeyer collected 201 studies on financial education: courses, classes, workshops. They asked one question: does any of it change what people do with their money?

Their answer was a number. Financial education explained about 0.1 % of the variation in the money behaviours studied. Not ten percent, not one, a tenth of one. The effect was weaker for people on low incomes, and it faded: even long programmes left almost no trace about 20 months later.1

The fair version

To be fair to teachers, the 2014 study mixed many kinds of research. In 2022 a newer analysis looked only at proper randomised experiments, 76 of them, and found that financial education does help, both knowledge and behaviour, by a modest amount similar to education in other subjects.2

So the honest summary: learning helps a little. But there's a wide gap between knowing something and doing it on a tired Tuesday evening. Tips live on the knowing side of that gap.

Why knowing isn't doing

The gap gets wider exactly when money is tight. A 2013 study in Science followed sugarcane farmers in India and tested the same people twice: before the harvest, when money was short, and after it, when they'd been paid. Before the harvest, the very same farmers scored clearly worse on thinking tests.3 They didn't become less clever. Their heads were full.

That matters, because worrying about money uses the same mind you need for planning money. When someone under money stress doesn't follow ten clever tips, that isn't laziness. And a tired mind takes the easy offer: buy now, pay later, minimum payment. Nobody has to plot that. It's simply where the incentives point.

So a good plan isn't the cleverest one. It's the one that asks the fewest decisions from a tired person.

What actually works

1. Change the default. A large US company changed one thing in its retirement plan. Before, new employees had to sign up. After, they were signed up automatically and had to act to leave. Participation among new hires went from 37 % to 86 %.4 Same people, same pay, same information. No tip did that. A default did.

2. Decide once, while you're calm. In the Philippines, a bank offered a savings account you couldn't touch until a date or goal you picked yourself. The paper is called "Tying Odysseus to the Mast", after the sailor who had himself tied up so he couldn't steer towards the Sirens. A year later, savings among people offered the account had risen about 81 % more than in the comparison group.5

3. Write an if-then rule. "If it's payday, then the transfer goes out first." "If something costs more than 100, then it waits two days in the cart." Across 94 studies, people who made plans in this if-then form were clearly more likely to do what they intended.6

4. Give it time. In a study of everyday habits, a new behaviour took a median of 66 days to feel automatic, anywhere from 18 to 254.7 If week three feels clumsy, nothing is wrong with you. You're early.

Your one-page plan

If it doesn't fit on one page, it's not a plan. It's a mood. Three lines are enough:

One page, three lines
  1. My one automatic move: a fixed transfer on payday, to savings or to the most expensive debt.
  2. My if-then rule: anything over 100 waits two days.
  3. My tip filter: who pays for this advice, what do they earn, and would it exist for free?

Then stop collecting tips. You almost certainly know enough already. What's missing isn't information. It's a system that runs on your worst day.
