The Intelligent Investor, Fact-Checked: Mr. Market and 4 More Rules vs the Evidence

Final score: 3½ / 5

Benjamin Graham's 1949 classic holds up better than most money books. Three of its five big ideas pass, one is complicated, and one fails: reading Mr. Market's mood to time the market. The surprise is that the book's best advice is its most boring part, and you may already be doing it.

There's a monster living in your portfolio. Some days he's grinning, some days he's screaming, and every day he shouts a new price at you. In 1949 Benjamin Graham gave him a name: Mr. Market.

The book is The Intelligent Investor. Warren Buffett read the first edition at nineteen and called it "by far the best book about investing ever written".1 We put five of its big ideas against real data. Each gets a verdict: true, half true or false. This is the written version of our Mr. Market video, with every source at the end.

Rule 1: Mr. Market is moody

Graham asks you to imagine you own a small share in a private business. Your partner, Mr. Market, "is very obliging indeed". Every day he tells you what your share is worth, and offers to buy you out or sell you more at that price.1 Example: your share cost $1,000. On a happy day he offers $1,400. On a scary day, $650. Same bakery, same bread. Only his mood changed.

Realistic? In 2020 the S&P 500 fell almost 34% between 19 February and 23 March, the fastest bear market on record. By 18 August it closed at a new record high.2 Most businesses didn't change nearly that much.

His price is an offer, not an order. But do people ignore him? Morningstar found that over the ten years to the end of 2025, funds returned about 9.9% a year, while the average investor's dollar earned about 8.7%, because of when people bought and sold.3 Example: $10,000 for ten years grows to about $25,700 at 9.9%, and about $23,000 at 8.7%. More than $2,600 lost to timing.

Verdict: TRUE. As Buffett wrote in 1987: "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you."4

Rule 2: Margin of safety

Asked to sum up sound investing in three words, Graham chose: margin of safety.1 Build a bridge for thirty tons, and only let ten-ton trucks cross. Pay clearly less than something is worth, so you can be wrong and still be fine, without needing an accurate forecast.

Example: you think a share is worth $100 and pay $70. It turns out to be worth $80. You're still 14% up. Pay the full $100 for the same mistake and you're down 20%.

Price matters for whole markets too. In 2012 Vanguard tested the signals people use to predict stock returns. The best was valuation, a ten-year price-to-earnings ratio, which explained about 40% of the differences in returns over the next ten years. Over one year, almost nothing worked.5 In 1992 Eugene Fama and Kenneth French showed that cheap stocks had earned higher average returns than expensive ones.6 But from 2007 to mid-2020 cheap stocks lagged badly, a drawdown of about 55%.7

Verdict: MOSTLY TRUE. A margin protects, it doesn't predict. For most of us it isn't a clever pick anyway. It's a cash buffer, a broad mix, and never needing to sell on a scary day.

Rule 3: Be a defensive investor

Graham splits his readers in two. The defensive investor wants good results without much time or worry. The enterprising investor is willing to do serious work. It's about attention, not money, and defensive is not second best. His recipe: high-grade bonds plus stocks spread across many large, solid companies.1

Buffett chose something similar for his own family. In his 2013 letter he described his instructions for the money his wife will inherit: 10% in short-term government bonds, 90% in a very low-cost S&P 500 index fund.8 Simple also helps behaviour. Morningstar found some of the smallest timing gaps among investors in all-in-one funds that mix stocks and bonds for them.3

To be fair, Graham's defensive investor picked a list of stocks, because index funds for ordinary investors only arrived in 1976.9 The spirit is the same: broad, cheap, and not dependent on your mood.

Verdict: TRUE. If you already invest automatically in a simple, broad fund and don't check it every day, you're already doing what this book asks.

Rule 4: Pick stocks that beat the market

Graham believed an enterprising investor could find bargains that beat the market. In 1984 Buffett described a group of Graham's students who each did it for years, in a talk called "The Superinvestors of Graham-and-Doddsville".10

That list was chosen after the fact. Brad Barber and Terrance Odean studied 66,465 households at a discount broker from 1991 to 1996. The market returned about 17.9% a year, the average household 16.4%, and the fifth who traded most just 11.4%.11 And in 1976, the year he died, Graham himself said: "I am no longer an advocate of elaborate techniques of security analysis in order to find superior value opportunities."12

The book's last line says it well: "To achieve satisfactory investment results is easier than most people realize; to achieve superior results is harder than it looks."1

Verdict: HALF TRUE (it's complicated). Possible, rare and hard to spot in advance. If you try, keep it to a small slice.

Rule 5: Time the market by his mood

If Mr. Market is moody, why not read his mood? Sell when he's euphoric, buy when he's terrified. Graham left a small door open: never less than 25% or more than 75% in stocks, leaning lower when prices look dangerously high.1

Cliff Asness, Antti Ilmanen and Thomas Maloney tested valuation timing on more than a century of data. The signal pointed the right way but was weak, and from 1958 to 2015 it did about the same as simply buying and holding.13

Example: you have $10,000. The market drops 20%, recovers 25%, then gains 10%. Stay put and you have $11,000. Sell at the bottom and buy back after the recovery, and you have $8,800. The best days often come right after the worst. J.P. Morgan calculates that missing the ten best days over twenty years would have cut an investor's yearly return by almost half.14

Verdict: FALSE. Mr. Market is a warning, not a signal. Let him shout.

The final score

Intelligent Investor 3½ / 5
  1. Mr. Market is moody: use him, don't obey him. True.
  2. Margin of safety: mostly true. It protects, it doesn't predict.
  3. Be a defensive investor: true.
  4. Pick stocks that beat the market: half true. Possible, rare and hard.
  5. Time the market by his mood: false.

Here's what surprised us. The book's most famous character is right as a warning and wrong as a trading signal. Its best advice is the boring part: be defensive, keep it simple, leave a margin. If you already do that, you're not behind. You're ahead of most investors.

Your small system this week

  1. Pick how often you'll look at your investments: monthly or quarterly. Every extra look is another chance to argue with Mr. Market.
  2. Count the moving parts in your plan. If you can't explain it in one sentence, simpler may be safer.
  3. On a calm day, write one sentence: "When the market drops, I won't sell. My automatic investing keeps running." Put it where you'll see it.
