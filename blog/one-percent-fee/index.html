What a "Tiny" 1% Fee Really Costs You (With the Math)

Verdict: "1% is small" · False

Over 40 years, a 1% yearly fee takes about a fifth to a third of the final result. It's the one part of investing you control completely. Check it once a year, in 15 minutes.

Two people invest the same 300 dollars a month for forty years, in the same market. The only difference between them is a number so small it fits in a footnote: one of them pays a fee of one percent a year.

How much less does that person end up with? Ten percent? Take a guess before you scroll.

The math the footnote hides

Example: at 7% a year, 300 dollars a month for 40 years grows to about $718,000. Pay 1% in fees, so you net 6%, and it grows to about $557,000. The difference is about $161,000, roughly 22% of the result.

With a single lump sum left alone, it's worse. A dollar at 7% for 40 years becomes about 15. At 6%, about 10. One percent a year costs about 31% of the outcome.

And it isn't just "one percent or zero". Same plan, different fees (Example, 7% before fees):

  • 0.1% fee → about $700,000
  • 0.5% fee → about $632,000
  • 1% fee → about $557,000
  • 2% fee → about $435,000

Every step on that ladder looks small. Together they're the price of a house.

Even the US regulator felt it had to spell this out. In an investor bulletin, the SEC showed that on $100,000 over 20 years at 4%, a 0.25% fee leaves you with about $209,000 and a 1% fee with about $181,000.1

Why fees hurt more than they look

Returns go up and down. Fees don't. In a year the market loses 10%, the fee still takes its 1%. And it takes it from the whole pot every year, not only from what you added, so the money it takes would have grown too. A fee is the only partner at the table who never has a bad year.

"But you get what you pay for"

That's the industry's best argument: pay more, get an expert who beats the market. The scorecard says otherwise. S&P Dow Jones Indices has compared actively managed funds with their own index for over twenty years. Over 15-year periods, roughly nine in ten actively managed US large-company funds have trailed the index.2 Not because the managers are foolish, but because they compete with each other and charge you for the contest.

In 1976, John Bogle launched a fund that simply bought every company in the index and did nothing. Wall Street called it "Bogle's folly". He hoped to raise 150 million dollars and raised about 11.3 Funds built on that idea now hold trillions. His advice fit in one line: don't look for the needle in the haystack, just buy the haystack.

The fee you charge yourself

There's one more fee that never shows up on a factsheet. Morningstar compares what funds returned with what their investors actually got, given when they bought and sold. In the ten years to 2023 the funds returned about 7.3% a year, and their investors got about 6.3%.4 The gap comes from timing: buying when things feel great and selling when they feel scary. The cheapest fund in the world can still cost you a percent a year if you sell it in a panic.

Your small system: the yearly Fee Check

One calendar entry a year, fifteen minutes. For each fund you own, write down three numbers:

  1. The ongoing charge (also called TER or expense ratio). It's always on the factsheet, usually in small print.
  2. Any entry or exit fee.
  3. What you pay for advice, if anything.

Add them up and multiply by your balance. That's this year's bill. If the ongoing charge is above half a percent, ask what you get for it that a broad, low-cost index fund wouldn't give you. If you decide to switch, start by sending the new monthly money to the cheaper fund. Selling old holdings can trigger tax in some countries, so check that first.

And write one more sentence, today: "When the market falls 20%, I will…" Finish it with "keep buying". That one line protects you from the fee you'd charge yourself.
