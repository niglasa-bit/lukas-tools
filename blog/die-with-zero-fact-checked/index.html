Die With Zero, Fact-Checked: Are You Saving Too Much?

Final score: 3½ / 5

Three of the book's five big ideas pass, one is complicated, and the one that fails is the title itself: nobody can plan to hit exactly zero. But the diagnosis holds. The people most at risk of wasting their money aren't the spenders. They're good savers who never give themselves permission.

Almost every money article says the same thing: save more, start earlier, never stop. Die With Zero, by energy trader and hedge fund manager Bill Perkins (2020), asks the opposite: what if you're saving too much?1 Its big idea is that money is only worth what it lets you do with your life, so plan to use it while you can still enjoy it. We tested five of its ideas against real data. This is the written version of our Die With Zero video, with every source at the end.

One note: this is for people who already save. If you're still building your first cushion, keep building.

Rule 1: Most people die with money they never used

The book's diagnosis: people save hard for decades, then never really spend it. The Employee Benefit Research Institute tested this in 2018, following American retirees over their first 18 years of retirement. At the median, those who started with under $200,000 spent down about a quarter of their savings. Those with $200,000 to $500,000 spent down 27.2%. Those with more spent down less than 11%. About a third of all retirees actually grew their savings. And retirees with a pension barely touched theirs: their savings fell about 4%, against 34% for retirees without one.2

Example: you retire with $1,000,000 and behave like the median retiree in the high-savings group. Eighteen years later you'd still have about $890,000.

A 2016 study by Chris Browning, Tao Guo, Yuanshan Cheng and Michael Finke found the same. Even after setting aside 40% of their assets for long life, health costs and heirs, the wealthiest retirees were spending far less than they safely could. What they skipped most wasn't rent or utilities. It was travel and eating out.3

Verdict: MOSTLY TRUE. Under-spending is mostly a problem of people who saved well. If that's you, the hard part is done. The next skill is permission.

Rule 2: Experiences pay a memory dividend

Perkins calls it the memory dividend: a great experience pays you when it happens, and again every time you remember it.1

In 2003, Leaf Van Boven and Thomas Gilovich found in two surveys that people were happier with their experiences than with the things they'd bought.4 Later work by Gilovich and Amit Kumar links part of this to people: experiences tend to be shared, and they connect us to others.5 And memory adds a bonus. Terence Mitchell and colleagues followed people on real trips, including a three-week bike tour in California. During the trip, people rated it lower than they'd expected. Afterwards, they remembered it as better than it was.6 The rain fades. The view stays.

The honest part: in 2018, Jacob Lee, Deborah Hall and Wendy Wood found that people with more resources got more happiness from experiences, while people with less got as much, or more, from useful things.7

Verdict: MOSTLY TRUE. It's strongest for people who already have a cushion. And a trip on a credit card is a memory with a monthly bill attached.

Rule 3: Spend more while you're healthy

Perkins suggests "time buckets": put each experience in the years you can enjoy it most.1 A hike at 75 isn't the same hike as at 55.

The health part is real. In US census data, 24% of people aged 65 to 74 report a disability, against 46% of those 75 and older. Serious difficulty walking or climbing stairs goes from 15% to 30%.8 And spending follows. In 2014 David Blanchett found retirees' spending fell about 1% a year after inflation on average, a shape he called the retirement spending smile, because near the end it turns back up.9

Example: you spend $60,000 a year at 65. Falling 1% a year after inflation, that's about $54,000 at 75 and $49,000 at 85.

But the upturn is real, and it's mostly health care. Mariacristina De Nardi, Eric French and John Bailey Jones found that medical costs rise steeply with age and income, and that for many older people the risk of living long and needing expensive care drives saving more than leaving money to heirs.10

Verdict: HALF TRUE (it's complicated). Give the active years a bigger budget, and keep a reserve for the end.

Rule 4: Give to your kids while you're alive

If you plan to leave money to your children anyway, why make them wait? An analysis of Federal Reserve survey data by the Penn Wharton Budget Model found the bulk of inheritances arrive between ages 46 and 75.11 The book puts the peak at around 60.1 Not when you're buying a first home or raising small kids.

Example: you give your child $50,000 at 30, and they clear a new ten-year loan at 7%. That's about $3,500 of interest they don't pay in the first year, and almost $20,000 over the whole loan.

The trade-off: the best gift to your kids is never needing their money later. So give from what's clearly beyond your own needs, not from your safety net.

Verdict: MOSTLY TRUE. Inheritances usually arrive late, when they help least. Secure your own floor first.

Rule 5: Aim to die with exactly zero

The title: spend down so your money and your life run out together. The problem: you don't know the date. Estimates vary, but for a healthy 65-year-old couple, the odds that at least one of them reaches 90 are at least even.12

Example: you retire at 65 with $800,000, earning 3% a year after inflation. To hit zero at 85 you could spend about $52,200 a year. Plan for 95 instead and it's about $39,600. Not knowing the date costs you about $12,600 a year of spending.

To its credit, the book sees this coming and points to annuities, insurance that pays you for as long as you live.1, 13 In theory economists agree: in 1965 Menahem Yaari showed that people who don't know how long they'll live should turn much of their savings into lifetime income.14 Few do, partly because of framing. Jeffrey Brown and colleagues found in 2008 that when an annuity is described as guaranteed spending rather than an investment, most people want it.15 A 2024 study by Blanchett and Michael Finke estimated that guaranteed lifetime income supports about twice as much spending as the same amount in savings, though it was published by an annuity industry group.16

Verdict: FALSE. You can't aim for an exact landing when you don't know where the runway ends. What works is the spirit: a secure floor of lifetime income, and the freedom to spend the rest.

The final score

Die With Zero 3½ / 5
  1. Most people die with money they never used: mostly true.
  2. Experiences pay a memory dividend: mostly true.
  3. Spend more while you're healthy: half true. Yes, but keep a reserve.
  4. Give to your kids while you're alive: mostly true.
  5. Aim to die with exactly zero: false.

So, are you saving too much? While you're working, probably not. But if you already save well, the bigger risk isn't running out. It's never spending your money on the life it was for. If that's you, you're not behind. You're ahead, and it's time to enjoy the view.

Your small system this week

  1. Write down your "enough" number. Without one, saving never turns into spending.
  2. List five experiences you want and the latest decade you could really enjoy each. "This decade" goes first.
  3. Compare the income you'll get for life, no matter what, with your basic costs. The bigger that floor, the freer the rest.
