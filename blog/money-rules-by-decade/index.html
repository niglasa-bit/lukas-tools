Money Rules for Your 20s, 30s, 40s and 50s: One Job per Decade

One job per decade

Same $300 a month, same fund, same 7 % a year. Start at 25 and you have about $718,000 at 65. Start at 45 and you have about $148,000. You can't change the decade you're in, but every decade has one job: 20s start, 30s automate, 40s fix the leaks, 50s aim. Do that job and the decade works for you.

Same $300, two very different results

Two people. Ella starts investing $300 a month at 25. Noah starts the same $300 at 45. Same boring index fund, same 7 % a year.1 At 65, Ella has about $718,000 and Noah about $148,000. Almost five times more, from the same monthly amount.

Ella didn't pick better funds or earn more. She paid in for twice as many months and got five times the result. That isn't skill. That's time.

If you're Noah, don't leave. The second half of this post is for you, and it's better news than that chart. This is the written version of our video on money rules by age, with the maths and every source at the end.

Your 20s: start

Here's the cruelest fact in personal finance. Ella invests $300 a month for ten years, from 25 to 35, then never adds another dollar. Someone else starts at 35 and invests the same $300 for thirty straight years. At 65, Ella's ten years still win: about $379,000 against about $340,000. She paid in a third as much. She just paid first.

The maths underneath is one number. At 7 % a year a dollar roughly doubles in ten years, becomes about four in twenty and about fifteen in forty. So a dollar invested at 25 is worth about fifteen at 65. A dollar invested at 45 is worth about four. Same dollar, more time to work.

And most people in their twenties haven't started. In the Federal Reserve's 2022 survey, only about half of households under 35 had any retirement account, and the typical balance among those who did was about $18,900.2 Doing anything puts you ahead of most. Charlie Munger put it bluntly: the first $100,000 is the hardest, then the money starts bringing in money.3

The job: start, even small. Fifty dollars a month is fine, because the habit is the asset. Use one broad index fund, not ten stocks. And use the Birthday Raise: every birthday, raise the monthly amount by $10. You'll never feel it.

Your 30s: automate

Your thirties are the decade of good reasons. A wedding, a move, a baby, a bigger car for the baby. Each is a perfectly good reason to pause the transfer "just until things settle". Things don't settle, and the three-month pause lasts seven years.

On the chart: Ella pauses from 32 to 38. She skips $25,200 of payments, and at 65 the hole is about $181,000, roughly seven times what she skipped. You skip the small payments and lose the big growth.

A useful yardstick from Fidelity: aim to have about one year's salary saved for retirement by 30, three by 40 and six by 50.4 It's a guideline, not a law. If you're 35 with zero, it doesn't mean "save more by willpower". It means make saving stop being a monthly decision.

The job: automate. Move the transfer to the morning of payday so it leaves before you see the balance. Split every raise: half goes to the transfer the same day, half is yours. And use the Pause Rule: if you must pause, put the restart date in your calendar before you stop. A pause without an end date is a cancel.

Your 40s: fix the leaks

The good news nobody tells you: for most people the forties are the best-paid decade. In US data, median weekly earnings are highest for workers aged 45 to 54, with 35 to 44 close behind.5

The bad news arrives at the same time. Spending peaks too: a bigger home, two cars, kids, parents who need help. The raise arrives and leaves in the same month. Economists call it lifestyle inflation. We call it the leak.

The forties have a second danger: being clever. Mark Twain was the most famous writer in America and at the top of his earnings when he poured his fortune into the Paige typesetting machine. It never worked. Accounts of his loss range from about $170,000 to $300,000, a fortune in today's money, and together with his failed publishing house it left him bankrupt in 1894. He spent years on a world lecture tour to pay his creditors back.6

Now back to Noah, who started at 45. His curve was the small one, but the forties have what the twenties didn't: income. If he invests $900 a month instead of $300, his result triples to about $443,000 at 65. Not Ella's number, but a very different retirement from nothing.

The job: fix the leaks. This month, list every monthly cost that didn't exist five years ago and cancel one. That's the Leak Audit. This year, use any extra room your retirement account gives older savers. And before you buy the house that eats the next twenty years, count its price in hours of your life.

Your 50s: aim

If the first chart felt like a door closing, here's the part we promised. Starting at 55 with $300 a month gives about $50,000 at 65. That's real money, but it isn't a retirement. So the job of this decade isn't catching up. It's aiming.

The years after sixty still count. Morgan Housel worked out that of Warren Buffett's $84.5 billion, $81.5 billion came after his mid-sixties.7 He didn't get smarter at 65. The money he'd built kept compounding while he was alive to see it. And if your fifties feel late for anything new: Harland Sanders signed his first Kentucky Fried Chicken franchise at 62.8

Aiming means two numbers. What you'll have: current savings, grown for ten or fifteen years, plus what you'll still add. What you'll need: yearly spending minus any pension, times the years. Most people in their fifties know neither, so fear fills the gap. Once both numbers exist, the fear turns into a plan, usually a smaller one than the fear.

The job: aim. Write both numbers down today, ugly is fine. Then use the three levers: save a bit more, spend a bit less in retirement, or work a bit longer. One extra working year counts twice, a year more in and a year less out. And use the Guardrail: if a product takes more than one sentence to explain, it isn't for your fifties.

  2. Start at 35: about $340,000. Job: automate.
  3. Start at 45: about $148,000. Job: fix the leaks.

The best decade to start was the last one. The second best is the one you're in.

How we calculated: $3,600 a year at 7 % a year, compounded yearly, FV = 3,600 × ((1.07n − 1) ÷ 0.07). Seven percent is roughly the long-run real return of US stocks. It's an assumption, not a promise; real returns vary a lot year to year.
