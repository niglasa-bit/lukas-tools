Same Salary, 15 Years Apart: Your Savings Rate Is Your Retirement Date

Final score: 1½ / 5

Five common retirement beliefs, tested with simple math. Only one and a half survive, and every myth that makes people feel behind fails. The number that moves your date most isn't your salary or your investments. It's the share of your pay you keep for future you.

Two people. Same age, same job, same paycheck to the dollar. One can stop working at 62. The other, if nothing changes, is still working at 77. Nobody picked better stocks and nobody inherited anything. The whole difference is one number. This is the written version of our video Same Salary, 15 Years Apart, with every source at the end.

All tables below are examples: 5% return a year after inflation, a target of 25 times yearly spending, starting from $0.

Number 1: Your savings rate

Your savings rate is how much of what you earn you keep for the future. Take your take-home pay, then add back anything invested before it reached your account, like your retirement plan contribution and your employer's match. What share of that went to future you?

Example: you take home $4,000 a month and $400 goes to your retirement plan before payday, so your real pay is $4,400. You also move $200 to an index fund. $600 ÷ $4,400 ≈ 14%.

Paying down a credit card counts, because it's money future you doesn't have to pay. Rent and car payments don't. And if your answer is zero, that's a row too. It's the top row, and the only way from there is toward an earlier date.

Number 2: Your number

Your number is the amount that lets your investments pay your bills. The classic rule of thumb is 25 times your yearly spending; we stress-tested it in our 4% rule video. The part almost nobody notices: your number depends on what you spend, not what you earn. So saving more works from both ends. More money goes in, and you get used to living on less, so the pile you need shrinks.

Number 3: The years in between

In 2012 the blogger Mr. Money Mustache published a table that went viral: starting from zero, the years to financial independence depend mostly on your savings rate.1 We rebuilt it with the assumptions above.

Savings rateYears to your number
5%66
10%52
15%43
20%37
25%32
30%28
50%17

Look at the jump from 10% to 15%: nine years. From 15% to 20%: another six. The first points you add are the most valuable you'll ever save. At 50%, every year of work buys a whole year of living; at 10%, it buys about six weeks. Real markets jump around year to year. The table uses the long-run average so you can see the shape.

Our two coworkers start at 25. One saves 10%, the other 20%: free at 77 versus free at 62. Those ages are before Social Security, which comes in below.

Myth 1: You need a high income

Look at the table again. There's no income column. A nurse and a surgeon who both save 20% reach their own number in the same 37 years; the surgeon's number is just bigger.

Ronald Read of Brattleboro, Vermont, worked as a gas station attendant and mechanic and later as a part-time janitor. When he died in 2014 his estate was worth almost $8 million, and he left about $4.8 million to the local hospital and $1.2 million to the library. His method, as his attorney described it, was a frugal life, shares in companies he understood, reinvested dividends, and decades of patience.5 One story doesn't prove a rule. We hear about him because it worked, which is exactly why the table uses a broad market average and not one person's luck.

Verdict: FALSE. Compare your rate with last year's you, not with anyone's salary.

Myth 2: Picking the right investments matters most

Returns matter. World stocks returned about 5.2% a year after inflation from 1900 to 2024, but only about 3.5% from 2000 to 2024.3 At 3% instead of 5%, the 10% saver waits 70 years instead of 52, eighteen years longer. The 50% saver waits 19 instead of 17. Returns matter a lot when you save a little, and very little when you save a lot. The market is the current; your savings rate is the rowing.

Verdict: IT'S COMPLICATED (½ point). Pick a simple, low-cost, broad index fund once, then put your energy into the rate.

Myth 3: If you start at 40, it's too late

From zero at 40, saving 25%, the table says 32 years: free at 72. But the table assumes your investments pay for everything. The Social Security Administration says benefits replace about 40% of pre-retirement income for an average worker.4 If a pension covers 40% of your spending, your number shrinks by 40% too. Rerun it and 25% from age 40 takes about 25 years: free at 65.

Savings rateYears, with ~40% covered
10%42
15%34
20%29
25%25

Forty is often your highest-earning stretch, and you already know what you actually want to spend money on, which makes a higher rate easier to keep.

Verdict: FALSE. Check your own estimate at ssa.gov/myaccount. For most people it makes the number they need smaller.

Myth 4: Save 15% and you'll be fine

Vanguard's 2026 report puts the average total savings rate, employer money included, at a record 12.1%, and 45% of savers raised their rate during the year.2 So at 15% you're already above the average saver. From your mid-twenties it lands you in your sixties even before Social Security. It won't get you to 50, and the table shows what would.

Verdict: TRUE, for a normal retirement age. Add one point now and one more at every raise. Start at 10% and three raises later you're at 13%, with your take-home pay up every single time.

Myth 5: You need $2 million

Example: you take home $50,000 a year and save 15%, so you live on $42,500. Twenty-five times that is about $1.1 million. Let Social Security cover 40% of that spending, and your investments need about $640,000.

And it keeps moving your way: pay off the house or see the kids move out, and your yearly spending, and your number, drop with it.

Verdict: FALSE. Your number belongs to your life, not to the internet.

What this means for a real paycheck

A typical full-time American earns about $62,000 a year, roughly $4,350 a month after federal and payroll taxes. Starting at 25, before any Social Security:

SavePer monthReach your number at about
10%$43577
15%$65068
20%$87062
25%$1,09057
30%$1,30553

Going from 15% to 20% is about $220 a month, and it moves your date by about six years. You don't need a bigger salary to move rows. You need one raise you don't spend.

The final score

Retirement myths vs math: 1½ / 5
  1. You need a high income: false.
  2. Picking the right investments matters most: it's complicated.
  3. If you start at 40, it's too late: false.
  4. Save 15% and you'll be fine: true, for a normal retirement age.
  5. You need $2 million: false.

If you already save something and invest it, you're doing the thing that actually moves the date. Near 12%, you're not behind. Above it, you're ahead of most people.
