Snowball, Avalanche or Baby Steps? What the Research Says About Paying Off Debt

Verdict: both work

The avalanche saves more interest. The snowball keeps more people going. In our example the difference is about 320 € and one month, much smaller than the difference between having a plan and not having one.

We paid off about 30,000 euros of debt at 12–20% interest with the debt snowball: smallest balance first. People who love spreadsheets will tell you that's the wrong order. They're right about the math. They're missing the human part. Here's both.

The two methods in one line each

Snowball: pay the minimum on everything, put every spare euro on the smallest balance. When it's gone, roll its payment into the next smallest. Popularised by Dave Ramsey's Baby Steps.1

Avalanche: same, but put every spare euro on the highest interest rate first.

Side by side

Example (not our numbers): three debts, 20,000 € in total, and 900 € a month for debt payments.

  • Car loan 3,000 € at 8%
  • Credit card 5,000 € at 20%
  • Consumer loan 12,000 € at 14%

Snowball (car loan first): first debt gone in month 7, all debt gone in month 27, total interest about 3,540 €.

Avalanche (credit card first): first debt gone in month 12, all debt gone in month 26, total interest about 3,220 €.

So the avalanche wins by about 320 € and one month. The snowball gives you a closed account five months sooner. Which of those matters more depends on you.

What the research says

A study of real debt-settlement clients found that people who closed off individual debt accounts were more likely to eliminate all their debt, whatever the size of those accounts.2 Seeing an account hit zero is a small victory, and small victories keep people going.

Another set of studies found that concentrating payments on one debt at a time, rather than spreading them, makes people feel more progress and work harder at repayment.3

The other side is real too: in experiments, people tend to pay off small debts first even when that costs them more, because closing accounts feels good.4 The researchers called it "winning the battle but losing the war".

Our honest summary: if numbers motivate you, go avalanche. If you need wins to keep going, go snowball. If the difference in your case is small, pick the one you'll actually stick to for years.

What worked for us

  • A full review first. Every income, expense and debt on one page.
  • A bare-minimum budget for a while. We sold the car. Every spare euro went to the smallest debt.
  • Consolidation at the end. Our own bank combined what was left into one loan at a lower rate.
  • A small monthly investment from the start. Mathematically it would have done more on the debt. But watching something grow every month made the long road feel lighter.
  • A budget that bends but doesn't break. Tax bills and family emergencies happen. Let the plan flex for a moment, then go back to it.
Consolidation, done right

Ask your own bank first. Compare the effective annual rate including all fees, not the headline rate. Check the total cost: a lower rate over many more years can still cost more. And close the cards you consolidated, or you can end up with the new loan and new card debt.

Your small system this week

  1. List every debt: balance, interest rate, minimum payment.
  2. Choose snowball or avalanche and write the order on one line.
  3. Set an automatic payment on payday: minimums on everything, the extra on number one.
  4. Put a date in your calendar when number one should hit zero. Celebrate it, cheaply.
