The 1955 Plan for How You Shop Kept 1 of Its 6 Promises

Final score: 1 / 6

Two promises come out half true, four are broken, and the last broken one is good news: you can opt out of the parts that make you poorer. The shopping playbook was designed by people, on purpose, in living memory. Anything designed can be redesigned, at least in your own house.

In the spring of 1955, a retail analyst named Victor Lebow published an article in a trade journal. One paragraph of it reads less like economics and more like an instruction manual for your life:

"Our enormously productive economy demands that we make consumption our way of life, that we convert the buying and use of goods into rituals, that we seek our spiritual satisfactions, our ego satisfactions, in consumption. […] We need things consumed, burned up, worn out, replaced, and discarded at an ever increasing pace."1

That was 1955. Now look at your phone: the notifications, the drops, the "only two left". A plan like that has to promise you something in return. We took its six biggest promises and checked each one against the evidence. This is the written version of our video on the 1955 playbook, with every source at the end.

Promise 1: Dissatisfaction drives progress

The playbook didn't start with Lebow. In January 1929, Charles Kettering, the head of research at General Motors, wrote a magazine article called "Keep the Consumer Dissatisfied".2 Kettering was an inventor, not a marketer: his team built the electric self-starter, so nobody had to crank a car by hand again. His point was that if everyone were satisfied with what they had, nobody would buy the better thing, and nobody would build it. He wasn't asking for things that break. He was asking for things that get better.

The problem is what came next. Point dissatisfaction at real improvement and you get the self-starter. Point it at the paint and the chrome and you get a new model every year that is the same car underneath. Over the decades that grew into a toolbox: a part chosen to wear out first, a new look every year, a charger that doesn't fit the old phone, a razor strip that changes color to tell you it's time.

You can watch the switch happen. For almost twenty years Henry Ford sold one car, the Model T, and barely changed how it looked. General Motors did the opposite: "a car for every purse and purpose", fresh styling, colors. By 1927 Ford shut down the Model T line. The lesson the industry took away: the car that lasts loses to the car that feels new.

One company proved you didn't have to play. In 1959 Volkswagen's American "Think Small" ads, by Doyle Dane Bernbach, bragged that the Beetle didn't change its looks for the sake of change, only to work better. Advertising Age later named it the best campaign of the twentieth century.3

Verdict: HALF. Dissatisfaction can drive progress. The playbook mostly used it to sell the same thing twice.

Your step: before any upgrade, finish this sentence: "The new one does ___, and I'll use that every week." If you can't fill the blank, it's chrome.

Promise 2: Buying will fulfil you

Notice Lebow's words: not "useful", not "fun", but spiritual and ego satisfaction. The promise is that buying fills something deeper. (Lebow was writing to store owners, and people still argue whether he was cheering this on or describing it with alarm. The promise itself can be tested.)

In 2014 Helga Dittmar, Rod Bond, Megan Hurst and Tim Kasser pooled the research on materialism and well-being: 259 independent samples. The result was consistent. The more central buying and owning are to someone's life, the lower their well-being tends to be, with an average correlation of about −0.19.4 Their explanation: people who put buying at the center tend to feel less free, less capable and less connected to others, and those three needs are what make a life feel full.

Honest note: it's a link, not proof, and the effect is modest. Unhappy people may also shop more. But nowhere in those 259 samples do you find the people for whom buying more made life deeper.

Verdict: BROKEN. Buying can make you comfortable. It doesn't make you fulfilled.

Your step: once a month, write down the three best moments of your month, then count how many of them you bought. For most people it's zero or one.

Promise 3: What you buy earns respect

Lebow again: "The measure of social status, of social acceptance, of prestige, is now to be found in our consumptive patterns."1 Economists have a clean way to test that: the lottery.

In 2020, Sumit Agarwal, Vyacheslav Mikhed and Barry Scholnick studied lottery winners in Canada, or rather their neighbors. The bigger the prize next door, the more the neighbors borrowed and the more likely they were to go bankrupt in the years after, and the spending went on things you can see from the street.5 A Dutch study of a postcode lottery found the same: when a street won, the neighbors who didn't win bought cars.6

Nobody in those studies earned respect. They earned debt. Think of the last time you saw a stranger's car and thought "that person must be wonderful". You didn't. Nobody does.

Verdict: BROKEN. Two countries, two lotteries, same result. The pressure is real; the respect never shows up. The bill does.

Your step: when a friend or neighbor upgrades, wait thirty days before you upgrade anything in the same category. The urge is theirs, not yours.

Promise 4: Credit lets you have the good life now

An "ever increasing pace" has one problem: paychecks don't grow at an ever increasing pace. So the playbook needed credit. Finland tested it as a whole country. In the late 1980s, tightly rationed bank lending was freed almost overnight, and lending grew by about 30% in a single year. Finns still call those years the casino economy. From 1991 the country fell into one of the deepest crises any rich country had seen since the 1930s, and unemployment went from about 3% to nearly 18%.7

Today the same tool is one tap away. Americans owe more than $1.2 trillion on credit cards, and people who carry a balance pay over 20% interest.8

The holiday ended years ago; the payments didn't. "Buy now, pay later" is the same idea with a friendlier button: small slices make a big price feel small.

Verdict: BROKEN. Credit doesn't let you have the good life now. It lets you have it now and pay for it twice.

Your step: if it will be used up before it's paid off, don't finance it. Houses and education are a different talk. Shoes, phones and holidays are not.

Promise 5: A richer society is a happier one

The biggest promise: keep the machine running and everyone gets richer and happier. Half of it came true. Americans are far richer per person than in 1955, and you can see it in the houses: the average new single-family home grew from about 1,660 square feet in 1973 to about 2,400 in 2024, while fewer people live in each one.9

But in 1974 economist Richard Easterlin noticed that as the United States got richer decade after decade, average happiness barely moved: the Easterlin paradox.10 It's still argued. Betsey Stevenson and Justin Wolfers found that richer countries are happier on average, and money clearly helps when you don't have enough.11 Above enough, extra stuff buys surprisingly little extra happiness. And "enough" is the one word the playbook doesn't have: an ever increasing pace never reaches it. That's not a bug in the plan. That's the plan.

Verdict: HALF. Richer, yes. Happier, not nearly as much as promised.

Promise 6: You can't opt out

The quiet promise: this is normal, it was always like this, you can't step out. But you've just watched it being built, in 1929, in 1955, in the 1980s, by people, on purpose. Your great-grandparents bought what they needed, saved the rest and repaired the rest of the rest, partly by choice and mostly because they had to.

Verdict: BROKEN, in your favor. You can opt out. Not of everything, but of the parts that make you poorer. The playbook works best on people who don't know it exists, and you now know the plan, the year it was written and the promises it broke.

The final score

The 1955 playbook 1 / 6
  1. Dissatisfaction drives progress: half.
  2. Buying will fulfil you: broken.
  3. What you buy earns respect: broken.
  4. Credit lets you have the good life now: broken.
  5. A richer society is a happier one: half.
  6. You can't opt out: broken, in your favor.

The Enough Line: your small system

You don't beat a system with willpower. Nobody built the playbook with willpower either; they built a system. So build a smaller one of your own and let it do the work.

  1. Write your enough list. The things you actually use and love. When one wears out, replace it with the best you can afford. Everything not on the list needs a reason.
  2. Turn off the dissatisfaction feed. Unsubscribe from store emails and shopping notifications. A want you never see is a want you never pay for.
  3. Pay the gap to future you. Every upgrade you skip, move what it would have cost into investing the same day. That turns every no into a yes to something.

Example: buy a $1,000 phone every four years instead of every two and you keep about $250 a year. Invested at 7% a year for 30 years, that's roughly $23,600, from one decision you make once. (An illustration, not a prediction.)

One thing for today: unsubscribe from three store emails before bed. Black Friday is the biggest shopping ritual of the year, and "ritual" is exactly the word Lebow used. Now you'll recognize it when you see it.
