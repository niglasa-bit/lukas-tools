Ben Franklin's The Way to Wealth, Fact-Checked: 5 Money Rules From 1758 vs Science

Final score: 3 / 5

Two of Franklin's rules pass, two are complicated, and his most famous line fails: "early to bed and early to rise" does not make you wealthy. The rule almost nobody quotes, think of saving as well as of getting, is the one the data backs most strongly.

The old man at the auction

A crowd waits for an auction to start. Someone asks an old man how anyone can afford these heavy taxes. He says the government isn't the biggest tax. "We are taxed twice as much by our idleness, three times as much by our pride, and four times as much by our folly."1

The old man is Father Abraham, a character Benjamin Franklin invented for his almanac in 1758. His speech gathers twenty-five years of Poor Richard's sayings, and it became famous on its own as The Way to Wealth.1 Franklin wasn't just preaching. At 42 he handed the daily running of his print shop to his partner David Hall and lived on his share of the profits.2

Does his advice still work? We tested five of Father Abraham's rules against modern data. This is the written version of our Ben Franklin video, with every source at the end.

Rule 1: Think of saving as well as of getting

"If you would be wealthy, think of saving, as well as of getting. The Indies have not made Spain rich, because her out-goes are greater than her incomes."1 He had a point. Spain shipped home mountains of American silver, and its king, Philip II, still defaulted on his debts four times. Two economic historians wrote a whole book about him: Lending to the Borrower from Hell.3

A modern version: Scott Hankins, Mark Hoekstra and Paige Skiba studied Florida lottery winners. Winning $50,000 to $150,000 only postponed bankruptcy. It didn't prevent it.4 And when Steven Venti and David Wise looked at American households near retirement who had earned about the same over their lives, their wealth was wildly different. The main reason wasn't luck or investment picks. It was how much they chose to save.5

Example: two people each earn $90,000 a year for thirty years and invest at 6% a year. One saves 15%, the other 5%. The first ends up with about $1.07 million, the second about $356,000. Same salary, a gap of more than $700,000.

A quick correction: "A penny saved is a penny earned" isn't Franklin's wording. In 1737 he wrote "A penny saved is two pence clear", which is even bolder.6

Verdict: TRUE. Income starts the race. Saving decides where you finish.

Rule 2: Beware of little expenses

"Beware of little expenses; a small leak will sink a great ship."1 But where does the money actually go? In the US government's spending survey for 2024, housing took 33.4% of the average household's spending, transportation 17.0% and food 12.9%. Those three alone are close to two-thirds.7

Example: cancel three forgotten $15 subscriptions and you keep $540 a year. Choose a home that costs 10% less than the average household's housing bill and you keep about $2,600. The big ship has big holes.

Still, the small leaks are real, and we're bad at seeing them. In a 2022 survey of 1,000 Americans for C+R Research, people underestimated their monthly subscription spending by $133 on average, and 42% were still paying for something they'd stopped using.8 For investors, the sneakiest small leak is often a fee, as we showed in What a "Tiny" 1% Fee Really Costs You.

Verdict: HALF TRUE (it's complicated). Leaks add up, but ships usually sink through the big holes: housing, cars and food. Fix those first, then go hunting for leaks.

Rule 3: He that goes a borrowing goes a sorrowing

"If you would know the value of money, go and try to borrow some; for he that goes a borrowing, goes a sorrowing."1

In 2013, researchers Richardson, Elliott and Roberts combined 65 studies with more than 34,000 people. People with unsecured debt had about three times the odds of a mental health problem.9 Another 2013 study, by Elizabeth Sweet and colleagues, looked at about 8,400 young American adults. Those who owed more relative to what they owned reported more stress, more depression and worse health.10

Example: $5,000 on a credit card at 22% interest. Pay $150 a month and it takes 52 months to clear. You pay about $2,800 in interest. That's sorrow with a receipt.

To be fair, these studies show a link, not proof that debt causes the stress, and a fixed-rate mortgage you can comfortably pay is a different animal from a growing card balance. Father Abraham adds a line every lender would sign: "Creditors have better memories than debtors."1

Verdict: MOSTLY TRUE. Not every loan is a sorrow, but the expensive kind usually is.

Rule 4: Time is money

"Dost thou love life? then do not squander time, for that is the stuff life is made of."1 Ten years earlier, in his 1748 Advice to a Young Tradesman, Franklin made it sharper: "Remember that time is money." If you can earn ten shillings a day and idle away half of it, you've also thrown away the five shillings you could have earned.11 The phrase was in print before him, but he made it famous.

It still works for decisions. Example: you take home $30 an hour and drive two hours across town to save $20. You just worked for $10 an hour.

The twist: in 2012, Sanford DeVoe and Julian House at the University of Toronto had some people think about their hourly wage, then played everyone a short piece of music. Those who had just priced their time enjoyed it less and felt more impatient.12

Verdict: HALF TRUE (it's complicated). Great for decisions, bad for weekends.

Rule 5: Early to bed, early to rise

The most famous line of all: "Early to bed, and early to rise, makes a man healthy, wealthy, and wise."1 First, it isn't really Franklin's. A version was printed in a book of English proverbs in 1639, almost a century before Poor Richard.13

Is it true? In 1998, Catharine Gale and Christopher Martyn tested it in the BMJ. They took 1,229 people aged 65 and over, picked out the larks and the owls, and followed them for 23 years. The larks weren't healthier, they didn't do better on cognitive tests, and they weren't wealthier. In fact the owls had the highest average income and were more likely to have a car.14

Verdict: FALSE. The line everyone quotes is the one with the least evidence behind it.

The final score

Way to Wealth 3 / 5
  1. Think of saving as well as of getting: true.
  2. Beware of little expenses: half true. The big holes are bigger.
  3. Borrowing brings sorrowing: mostly true.
  4. Time is money: half true. For decisions, not for living.
  5. Early to bed makes you wealthy: false.

The speech ends with a line we love. The crowd "heard it, and approved the doctrine, and immediately practised the contrary", because the auction opened and they began to buy.1 If you save a steady share of your income, avoid expensive debt and watch the big holes, you're not that crowd. You're ahead of an empire that had all the silver in the world.

Your small system this week

  1. Work out last month's saving rate: what you kept divided by your take-home pay. Write it down.
  2. Sort last month's bank statement by size, not date. Look at the top three lines first, then cancel one forgotten charge.
  3. Drop the alarm-clock guilt and set one automatic transfer for payday. It works while you sleep, whenever that is.
