The Richest Man in Babylon, Part 2: The 5 Laws of Gold vs Modern Evidence

Final score: 3½ / 5

Three laws hold up, one is complicated, and one fails in a way we didn't expect. The tablet is right that gold slips away from people who invest in what they don't understand. But its test, "stick to what you're familiar with", is exactly what modern research warns against.

A bag of gold and a clay tablet

A rich man in Babylon gave his son two things: a bag of gold, and a clay tablet with five laws carved into it. The son lost the gold anyway.

The story is from The Richest Man in Babylon, written in 1926. We tested its seven cures for a lean purse in part one. This is the chapter where the money gets lost. Around a campfire, the camel trader Kalabab tells how Arkad sent his son Nomasir away for ten years with the gold and the tablet. Nomasir lost part of the gold on a rigged horse race in Nineveh, and more in a business with a partner he barely knew. Only then did he read the tablet.1

This chapter is about not losing what you already have. We put each law against modern evidence. This is the written version of our Laws of Gold video, with every source at the end.

Law 1: Not less than one-tenth

"Gold cometh gladly and in increasing quantity to any man who will put by not less than one-tenth of his earnings to create an estate for his future and that of his family."1

Good news first. Vanguard's 2026 How America Saves report puts the average total savings rate in its workplace plans, what workers put in plus what employers add, at about 12% of pay.2 The typical saver is already past Clason's floor. And an employer match is the closest thing to gold coming gladly that modern life offers.

But is a tenth enough to retire on? Researchers at Boston College's Center for Retirement Research put the figure for a typical household closer to 15%.3

Example: you take home $3,000 a month. Ten percent is $300 a month, which over thirty years at an assumed 5% a year grows to about $250,000. At 15%, it's about $375,000.

Verdict: MOSTLY TRUE. In fairness to Clason, he never said exactly ten. He said not less than ten. The tablet was always a floor, not a ceiling.

Law 2: Profitable employment

"Gold laboreth diligently and contentedly for the wise owner who finds for it profitable employment, multiplying even as the flocks of the field."1

In the 2026 edition of Elroy Dimson, Paul Marsh and Mike Staunton's yearbook of returns since 1900, one dollar put into American stocks in 1900 grew to about $3,300 by the end of 2025, after inflation. The same dollar in short-term Treasury bills, basically cash, grew to under $2.4 The only difference was whether it had a job.

Example: $10,000 for thirty years. At half a percent a year above inflation it ends up around $11,600. At an assumed 5% above inflation, about $43,200.

The fine print: US stocks fell by more than half more than once along the way. Emergency money belongs in cash, long-term money in the field.

Verdict: TRUE. Gold that works multiplies. Gold that sleeps mostly just keeps up.

Law 3: The advice of men wise in its handling

"Gold clingeth to the protection of the cautious owner who invests it under the advice of men wise in its handling."1 In 2012, Sendhil Mullainathan, Markus Noeth and Antoinette Schoar sent mystery shoppers to real financial advisers around Boston. The advisers mostly didn't fix bad habits, and nudged even shoppers with a sensible portfolio toward more expensive actively managed funds.5 A later study of Canadian advisers found most invested their own money the same way they advised clients: frequent trading, expensive funds. They believed it.6

The other side: good advice can stop you selling everything in a crash. Vanguard, which also sells advice, estimates a good adviser can add up to about 3%, with behavioural coaching the biggest part.7

Verdict: HALF TRUE (it's complicated). Advice can protect you from yourself. But plenty of it costs more than it adds, and you can't tell by the suit.

Law 4: Businesses with which he is not familiar

"Gold slippeth away from the man who invests it in businesses or purposes with which he is not familiar or which are not approved by those skilled in its keep."1

The warning is easy to confirm. DALBAR found the average stock fund investor earned 16.54% in 2024, while the S&P 500 returned 25.02%. In 2025 the gap almost closed: 17.16% against 17.88%.8 Over ten years, Morningstar finds investors earned about 1.2 points a year less than their funds, with the biggest gaps in the most volatile funds.9 Example: $500 a month for thirty years is about $610,000 at 7%, and about $483,000 at 1.2 points less. A 2026 study argued the real timing cost is closer to 0.1% a year.10 The size is debated. The direction isn't.

But the cure, stick to what's familiar, backfires. In 2001 Gur Huberman found that people tended to own shares of their own local phone company, the name they saw on their bill.11 The most familiar business of all is your employer. At the end of 2000, 62% of the money in Enron's 401(k) plan was in Enron stock. A year later, shares that had traded above $80 were worth less than a dollar.12 Knowing the company didn't protect the employees.

Verdict: FALSE. Not the warning, the test. Familiarity feels like knowledge, and it makes people put too much in one place. Familiar isn't the same as understood.

Law 5: Impossible earnings

"Gold flees the man who would force it to impossible earnings or who followeth the alluring advice of tricksters and schemers or who trusts it to his own inexperience and romantic desires in investment."1

In 1920, six years before the book, Charles Ponzi promised Boston investors 50% in 45 days.13 Compounded for a year, that's more than 2,500%: $1,000 would become about $26,800. Stocks, remember, earned roughly 6½% a year after inflation. He paid early investors with new investors' money, and by August 1920 it collapsed.

The tricksters still win. Americans reported losing $15.9 billion to fraud in 2025. Investment scams were the biggest category, at over $7.9 billion, with an average loss of over $10,000. The largest losses went out by bank transfer and cryptocurrency, and the real total is higher, because not everyone reports.14

Verdict: TRUE. The cleanest law on the tablet. The tricksters haven't changed their promise. Only their phone number.

The final score

Laws of Gold 3½ / 5
  1. Save not less than a tenth: mostly true. A great floor, aim for fifteen.
  2. Put your gold to work: true.
  3. Take advice from the wise: half true. Ask how they're paid.
  4. Avoid what you're not familiar with: false. Right warning, wrong test.
  5. Beware impossible earnings: true.

Nomasir lost his gold to laws four and five, the two about trusting the wrong thing. Protecting is where most people slip, not because they're foolish, but because the wrong thing often feels the most familiar or the most exciting. If you save something, keep long-term money working and pause before every exciting offer, you're ahead of Nomasir after his first year.

Your small system this week

  1. Find your savings rate on your last pay slip and add anything your employer puts in. Below ten? Move up one percent today.
  2. Before taking advice, ask two questions: how exactly are you paid, and must you always act in my best interest?
  3. Write one rule where you'll see it: any offer with high returns, low risk and a reason to hurry gets a 48-hour wait and one call to someone you trust.
