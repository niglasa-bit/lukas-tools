Pharmacists Buy the Brand Only 9% of the Time: Store Brand vs Name Brand

Final score: 1½ / 6

"You get what you pay for" survives in one case and a half out of six. Price is a weak hint of quality, and the people who know what is inside the box are the ones who refuse to pay for the box. The one belief that holds is the fair one: some things really are worth paying for.

Two boxes of headache pills. Same active ingredient, same dose. One costs several times as much. Which one does a pharmacist take home?

Four economists answered that with data from more than 70 million shopping trips. Pharmacists picked the national brand only 9% of the time. The average shopper picked it 26% of the time.1 So we put the most expensive sentence in shopping on trial: "you get what you pay for". Six things most of us believe about price and brands, each checked against the evidence. This is the written version of our video, with every source at the end.

Belief 1: A higher price means higher quality

For decades researchers have done something simple: take the lab scores from consumer testing magazines, put them next to the prices and measure how closely the two move together. A perfect guide would give a correlation of 1. A useless one would give 0.

The average across those studies comes out at about 0.27.2 That is not nothing: pricier products do test a little better on average. But it is weak, weak enough that in plenty of categories the cheaper product scored higher.

The reason is that a price isn't set by the factory. It is set by what the seller thinks you will pay. A strong brand, a nice box and a good spot on the shelf can carry a much higher price than the thing inside costs to make. The price tells you about the market. It only whispers about the product.

Verdict: HALF. Price is a signal of quality, and a weak one.

Your step: treat price as a rumor, not a fact. When it matters, look up an independent test before you look at the tag.

Belief 2: Store brands are for people who can't afford better

The feeling arrives the second you see the label: cheap feels a little embarrassing, expensive feels a little safer. Nobody tasted anything. So look at who actually buys store brands.

The same study that looked at pharmacists looked at chefs. Professional cooks put about 12 percentage points less of their pantry spending (salt, sugar, flour, baking soda) into national brands than people with the same income and background. And it isn't about money. Shoppers who could correctly name the active ingredient in a painkiller bought the store brand 85% of the time, 19 points more than shoppers who couldn't. Same income, more information.1

It isn't a niche either. In Europe, store brands made up 38.8% of supermarket sales by value in 2025, and more than half in Switzerland.3

Verdict: FALSE. Experts buy store brands more, not less. Store brands are for people who read the back of the box.

Your step: next time, read the ingredient list on the brand and the store version side by side. If it is the same list, you just found free money.

Belief 3: You can taste the difference

In the early 1960s two marketing researchers gave loyal beer drinkers unlabeled bottles. Blind, they rated the beers about the same, and most could not pick out their own brand. With the labels back on, the ratings changed, and everyone's favorite tasted best.4

Forty years later scientists put the cola war inside a brain scanner. Blind, preferences between the two big colas split roughly down the middle. When a picture of the can appeared before the sip, preference for one of the brands jumped, and so did activity in brain areas linked to memory.5 The drink didn't change. The story arriving with it did.

Wine is the big one. In more than 6,000 blind tastings, ordinary drinkers did not enjoy more expensive wine more. If anything they liked it slightly less. Trained tasters were the exception.6

The honest twist: in another experiment people tasted the same wine twice, once labeled $5 and once labeled $45. They said the $45 one tasted better, and the brain's pleasure response agreed.7 The price doesn't just describe the experience. It changes it.

Verdict: FALSE. Mostly you taste the label. That is good news, because you can test it.

Your step: run a blind test at home on one thing you buy every week. Someone else pours, you guess. If you can't tell, the cheaper one wins for good. If you can, keep the one you love and enjoy it.

Belief 4: The brand tells you who made it

A few hundred years ago the name on a product was the workshop that made it. Global manufacturing broke that link. Today the company on the label often designs, markets and sells, while a specialist somewhere else makes the thing at scale, often for several competing brands at once.

Picture a sunglasses shop: Ray-Ban, Oakley, Persol, Oliver Peoples, Vogue Eyewear. Five brands, five moods, one owner: EssilorLuxottica. The same group makes eyewear under licence for fashion names such as Prada, Miu Miu, Burberry, Dolce&Gabbana and Michael Kors, and it runs shops you would buy them in, like Sunglass Hut and LensCrafters.8

A brand can also change factories without changing anything you see. Bulgari's glasses were made by Luxottica for more than twenty years. Since 2024 they are made by Thélios, the eyewear company owned by LVMH.9 Same name on the arm, different company behind it.

Laptops work the same way: the large majority of the world's notebooks are assembled by a handful of Taiwanese contract manufacturers most buyers have never heard of, and the logo goes on at the end.10 In groceries it sometimes happens in plain sight: Costco's own-label house blend coffee says "roasted by Starbucks" on the bag.11

None of this is a scandal. Contract manufacturing is efficient, and it is one reason quality is far more even than it was two generations ago. It just means the logo is a story about the product, not a map of where it came from.

Verdict: FALSE. The brand tells you who designed it, who marketed it and who you will complain to. That is worth something, just not always the extra you are paying.

Your step: when the price gap is big, spend thirty seconds searching who makes the thing.

Belief 5: Some things really are worth paying for

Before you throw every brand out of the house: the opposite idea, that cheap is always smarter, is just as wrong as the price tag.

Quality hasn't disappeared, it has moved. At the very bottom of the price range the difference is usually obvious: thin, flimsy, broken in a week. In the middle the differences get hard to see, and that is where independent tests earn their keep. At the very top you pay a lot more for a little more.

And a brand can be a real promise: a warranty, spare parts, a safety record, someone who has to answer if it goes wrong. For things that protect your body, or that you use for hours every day, paying for proven quality is often money well spent.

Verdict: TRUE. Smart isn't the cheapest choice. It is the choice that matches the job.

Your step: split your shopping into two lists. Same-thing products, where the cheapest good one wins. Hard-use products, where you read the tests first.

Belief 6: More features means a better buy

There is a famous line in marketing, popularized by Harvard professor Theodore Levitt: people don't want a quarter-inch drill, they want a quarter-inch hole. Levitt credited it to an advertising man, Leo McGivena, and versions of it appeared in newspaper ads in the 1940s.12

Every store is built to sell you the drill: feature lists, comparison charts, the "pro" version right next to the normal one. But almost every purchase is really a hole. You want to get to work. You want hot coffee. You want photos of your kids.

So before a bigger purchase, ask three questions. What hole am I trying to make? What is the simplest thing that makes that hole well? Do I need to own it, or just use it once? Surprisingly often the answer is the mid-range model, a used one, or one borrowed from a friend.

Verdict: FALSE. More features are a better buy only if you will use them. Buy the result, not the feature list.

The final score

The price tag 1½ / 6

  2. Store brands are for people who can't afford better: false.
  3. You can taste the difference: false.
  4. The brand tells you who made it: false.
  5. Some things really are worth paying for: true.
  6. More features means a better buy: false.

The Swap List: your small system

None of this needs willpower. The shelf, the price tag and the logo are a system someone else designed for you. Don't fight it in the aisle every week. Replace it with a small system of your own, once.

  1. Write down ten things you buy every week.
  2. Test each one once. Read the two labels side by side, or run a blind test.
  3. Same? Swap for good. Every item where you can't tell the difference goes on the list as a permanent swap. You never decide again.

The store will redesign the shelf next month: new packaging, new "premium" lines, new offers at eye level. Your list already knows the answer.

Example: say the swaps save $15 a week. That is $780 a year. Set aside every year for ten years at 7%, it grows to about $10,800, for a shopping list you wrote once. (An illustration, not a prediction.)

One thing for today: pick one product you buy every week and read the back of the brand and the store version side by side. That is item one on your Swap List. And if you already buy store brands, you have been shopping like a pharmacist all along.
